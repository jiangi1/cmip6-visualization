Our Earth is warming

Since 1850, global temperatures have risen about 1.2°C. Scientists project continued warming through 2100, with the Arctic warming 2–3× faster than the rest of the world.

⚡

The good news? It depends on our emissions choices today.

Future warming varies dramatically — from 1.5°C to 4.5°C — depending entirely on which emission pathway humanity chooses to follow.

Where We Stand Today

In 2015, the world signed the Paris Agreement. Global temperatures had already risen roughly 1°C above pre-industrial levels, with the warming signal unmistakable in every region. Scientists were now certain: the planet would keep warming — but how much depended entirely on future choices.

2015 · The Fork in the Road

Three Futures, One Decision

CMIP6 models three distinct emission pathways. SSP1-2.6 imagines a world that rapidly decarbonizes, keeping warming close to 1.5°C. SSP2-4.5 follows current policy trends toward roughly 2.7°C. SSP5-8.5 assumes fossil fuel growth continues unchecked — a worst-case 4–5°C world by 2100.

🌱 SSP1-2.6 — ~1.5°C by 2100
⚠️ SSP2-4.5 — ~2.7°C by 2100
🔥 SSP5-8.5 — ~4–5°C by 2100

2030 · SSP2-4.5 Medium

The 2030s: Early Divergence

By 2030, all three scenarios still look similar — greenhouse gas inertia means early differences are small. But the Arctic is already responding faster than the rest of the planet, warming roughly 2–3× the global average. CMIP6 models consistently show polar amplification beginning well before mid-century.

❄️ Arctic warming 2–3× faster than global mean
🌊 Sea level rise already locked in from past emissions
🗺️ 2030 · Medium Emissions (SSP2-4.5)
❄️ Step 4 of 10
2040 · SSP5-8.5 High

The Arctic in Crisis

Under high emissions (SSP5-8.5), CMIP6 models project the Arctic Ocean could see its first ice-free September as early as the late 2040s. The loss of sea ice triggers a feedback: dark ocean water absorbs more sunlight than reflective ice, accelerating warming further. This is polar amplification in action.

❄️ SSP5-8.5: ice-free Arctic September by late 2040s
🌊 SSP1-2.6: ice loss limited to ~65% in September

2060 · SSP5-8.5 High

Crossing 2°C — A Critical Threshold

Under high emissions, CMIP6 models project the global mean temperature crossing 2°C above pre-industrial levels around mid-century. This is the upper guardrail of the Paris Agreement. Beyond it, IPCC assessments find substantially higher risks: more extreme heat events, disrupted monsoons, and accelerating sea level rise.

🌡️ SSP5-8.5 crosses 2°C around 2040–2050
🌊 Every 0.5°C matters: risk increases non-linearly

The Same Year — A Different World

In the same year 2060, the low-emissions pathway shows a dramatically different picture. Under SSP1-2.6, global warming stabilizes near 1.5°C as CO2 emissions approach net zero. Compare the map to the previous step — the color difference tells the story of what human choices can prevent.

2080 · SSP2-4.5 Medium

Uneven Warming: Who Bears the Burden?

CMIP6 shows warming is deeply unequal. High-latitude regions like Canada, Russia, and Scandinavia warm 1.5–2× the global average. Meanwhile, tropical and equatorial regions — home to billions of people with fewer resources to adapt — face longer, more intense heat seasons and disrupted rainfall patterns.

🧊 Russia & Canada: up to 1.8–2× global warming rate
☀️ Tropical regions face prolonged heat seasons

2100 · SSP5-8.5 High

End of Century: The High-Emissions World

Under unchecked fossil fuel growth, CMIP6 projects 4–5°C of global warming by 2100. Sea levels could rise over a meter. Arctic summers become ice-free for five months of the year. Large portions of the tropics experience conditions dangerous for outdoor labor. This is the world we leave if emissions are not reduced.

🌊 Sea level rise: up to 1m+ projected under SSP5-8.5
❄️ Arctic ice-free for 5 months per year by 2100

End of Century: The Livable World

Under low emissions, the world of 2100 is recognizable — still warming, but at ~1.5°C above pre-industrial. The most catastrophic tipping points are avoided.
